Common Ecommerce SEO Mistakes That Hold Online Stores Back

Most stores are not held back by one dramatic error, but by a handful of ordinary mistakes repeated across many pages. Here are the ones that appear most often, why they matter, and how to fix them in the right order.

By Zaheer SEO Expert · 27 October 2026 · Ecommerce SEO

When an online store struggles in search results, owners often assume they need some advanced technique. In practice, the causes are usually basic and familiar: pages with almost nothing on them, text copied from suppliers, filters creating thousands of weak page copies, and important pages Google has never found. The good news is that these mistakes are fixable, and fixing them improves the store for shoppers at the same time. This article walks through the most common ones so you can check your own store honestly.

If you find several of these in your shop, do not panic and do not try to repair everything overnight. Work through them in the order below, starting with your best-selling categories and products. A structured review like our ecommerce SEO service follows the same order.

Mistake 1: Thin Product Pages

A thin product page contains a title, a price, one image and perhaps a single sentence. It gives Google almost nothing to judge the page on, and gives the shopper almost nothing to base a decision on. Stores often end up with thin pages because products were uploaded in bulk from a spreadsheet. The fix is not to write essays, but to add the essentials covered in our product page SEO guide: a clear unique title, a real description, proper specifications and more than one helpful image, starting with the products that matter most.

Mistake 2: Duplicate Descriptions Everywhere

Copying the manufacturer's description feels efficient, but it means your page says exactly what many other stores say. Google then has little reason to show your version, and shoppers learn nothing about your store. The same problem happens inside one store when similar products share one copied paragraph with only the colour changed. Rewrite descriptions in your own words, using the facts but adding your knowledge of the product and your customers. Even a modest original description beats an identical polished one.

Mistake 3: Faceted Navigation Out of Control

Filters and sorting options are helpful for shoppers, but each combination of colour, size, brand and price can create a new page address. Left unmanaged, a store can generate a huge number of near-identical pages that waste Google's crawling effort and dilute the strength of the real category pages. The remedy is to keep filters for visitors while controlling which filtered addresses search engines should index, as explained in our category page SEO guide. Only meaningful groupings that people actually search for deserve to be standalone pages.

Mistake 4: Ignoring How the Store Works on Mobile

Many owners check their store on a desktop computer, while most of their visitors browse on phones. Small text, buttons placed too close together, slow-loading images and a checkout that fights the user will quietly lose sales and send poor signals about the experience. Test the full journey on a real phone: find a product, filter a category, add to cart and check out. If anything feels like hard work to you, it feels worse to a new visitor who has no reason to persist.

Mistake 5: Deleting Pages Without a Plan

Products sell out and lines end, and the instinct is often to delete the page immediately. But a page that shoppers still find through search or old links then leads to a dead end, and any value it built is lost. Temporary stock gaps usually call for keeping the page with a clear out-of-stock message, while discontinued items may be better redirected to a close alternative or the parent category. Deleting without thinking is the mistake; having a simple policy for stock changes prevents it.

Mistake 6: Treating Every Page the Same

A subtler mistake is spreading effort evenly, as if a minor accessory page matters as much as the category your store is known for. When everything is a priority, nothing is. Your best products and main categories deserve complete descriptions, the best images and regular reviews of their accuracy, while minor pages can follow a simpler standard until time allows. Stores with limited time, which is most stores, get far better results by ranking their pages in order of importance and working down the list, instead of making small, scattered improvements that never quite finish any single page properly.

Mistake 7: Never Checking What Google Sees

The final mistake is working blind. Free tools show which of your pages Google has indexed, which searches bring visitors, and where errors are appearing. Stores that never look at this information can carry broken pages, blocked sections or missing products for months without knowing. Build a small habit of checking, and most of the mistakes in this article become visible early, while they are still cheap to fix.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which ecommerce SEO mistake is most damaging?

There is no single worst mistake, but thin, duplicated product pages affect many stores at once because they cover so much of the site. Fixing the descriptions and titles of your most important products usually gives the clearest early improvement in page quality.

How can I check if Google has indexed my product pages?

Use Google Search Console, which is free. It shows which pages are indexed and which are excluded, along with reasons such as duplicate content or crawl problems. Checking it regularly helps you catch mistakes before they grow.

Is it bad to delete products I no longer sell?

Deleting is sometimes right, but do it thoughtfully. A discontinued product with search interest may be better redirected to a close replacement or its category, while a page nobody visits and nothing links to can usually be removed without harm.

How often should I review my store for these mistakes?

A practical rhythm is to check new products as they are added, review your main categories regularly, and run a fuller check a few times a year. Mistakes creep in as stock and staff change, so light, regular attention prevents big clean-ups.
