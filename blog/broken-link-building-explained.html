Broken Link Building Explained: Turning Dead Links Into Useful Ones

Every older website has links that no longer work. Broken link building means finding them, helping the owner fix the problem, and suggesting a useful replacement. Here is how the technique works, step by step.

By Zaheer SEO Expert · 27 October 2026 · Off-Page SEO

Broken link building sounds like a trick, but the idea is straightforward and helpful. Websites change over time. Pages get deleted, businesses close, and links that once worked start leading to error pages. Those dead links are called broken links. Broken link building means finding them, telling the site owner, and suggesting a suitable replacement page, sometimes one of yours, that their readers can use instead.

Everyone benefits when it is done well. The site owner fixes an embarrassment on their page. Their readers reach useful content instead of an error. And if your page is genuinely the right replacement, you earn a relevant link. This guide explains the process step by step.

Why Broken Links Exist Everywhere

The web is constantly being rearranged. A business rebuilds its site and old addresses disappear. A blogger recommends a tool that later shuts down. A news site links to a report that gets moved. Over months and years, a share of the links on almost every older page quietly stop working. Site owners rarely notice, because nothing alerts them when a page they linked to years ago vanishes.

That is why this technique exists. You are offering free quality control. As part of wider off-page SEO and link building work, it is one of the more welcome forms of outreach, because your first message is genuinely useful even if the owner never links to you.

How the Process Works

  1. Find resource pages in your field or area that link out to many helpful sites.
  2. Check their links for ones that lead to error pages or closed businesses.
  3. Prepare a replacement page on your site that covers the same topic well.
  4. Contact the owner politely, point out the dead link, and suggest your page as one possible fix.

Notice the order. Your replacement page must exist and be genuinely good before you write to anyone. Linking a thin or unrelated page wastes the owner's time and damages your credibility. If the dead link pointed to a guide, your replacement should be a guide of similar or better quality.

Finding Broken Links Without Expensive Tools

Professional tools can scan pages automatically, but beginners can start manually. Visit resource pages, link lists and older blog posts in your niche, and click through their recommendations. Free browser extensions can highlight dead links on a page. Local angles work well too: old lists of area businesses or suppliers often contain links to firms that have closed or moved.

Keep a note of each dead link you find, the page it sits on, and what it used to point to. Archived copies of the missing page, where available, help you understand what content the owner originally wanted to recommend, so your suggestion matches their intent.

Writing an Outreach Message That Gets Replies

Keep it short and helpful. Say who you are, name the exact page, and point out which link no longer works. Then offer your page as a possible replacement, while making clear the owner is free to simply remove the dead link instead. That lack of pressure is important. Many owners fix the link and choose your page because it is convenient and relevant, not because they were pushed.

Never send the same message to hundreds of sites, and never invent broken links to get attention. Site owners check, and dishonesty ends the conversation. Our overview of off-page SEO strategies shows where this technique sits alongside other honest methods.

What Results to Expect

Broken link building is a numbers game with modest returns. Many messages go unanswered, because sites are abandoned or owners are busy. A share of owners fix the link without using your page. Some do use it, and those links tend to be relevant and durable, since your page now serves a real purpose on theirs.

Treat the technique as one steady channel among several, not a complete strategy. Combined with good listings, helpful content and genuine relationships, it adds links that make complete sense to anyone reviewing them, which is exactly the kind of link profile that ages well.

A Final Note on Manners

This method works because it starts with helping. You are pointing out a fault the owner would want to know about. Keep that spirit in every message, accept refusals gracefully, and never follow up aggressively. Reputation travels, especially in smaller business communities, and the goodwill you build can matter as much as any single link.

Keeping Records of Your Outreach

Keep a simple record of every broken link you report: the site, the page, the date you wrote and any reply. This stops you contacting the same owner twice about the same link, which looks careless, and it lets you follow up once, politely, if you hear nothing after a couple of weeks. One follow-up is reasonable; more than that becomes pestering. Your records also show you which kinds of sites respond best, so over time your effort goes where it earns results instead of being spread thinly everywhere.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is broken link building?

It is a link building method where you find links on other websites that lead to deleted or error pages, inform the site owner, and suggest a suitable working page, sometimes your own, as a replacement their readers can use.

Do I need paid tools for broken link building?

No. Paid tools speed up checking, but you can start manually by visiting resource pages in your field and clicking their links, or by using a free browser extension that highlights dead links on a page.

What makes a good replacement page?

Your page should cover the same topic as the missing page, at similar or better quality, so the site owner can swap the link without disappointing readers. A thin or unrelated page will almost always be rejected.

Is broken link building safe for SEO?

Yes, when done honestly. The link is earned by helping fix a real problem and pointing to relevant content. It only becomes a problem if you spam site owners or mislead them, which is why polite, accurate outreach matters.
