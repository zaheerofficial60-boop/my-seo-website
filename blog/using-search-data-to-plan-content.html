Using Search Data to Plan Content Your Customers Want

The best content ideas are not guesses. They are sitting in your Search Console data, in the real words customers typed. This guide shows a simple process for turning queries into topics.

By Zaheer SEO Expert · 27 October 2026 · Analytics & Reporting

Many businesses decide what to write about by guessing, copying competitors, or writing whatever comes to mind on a quiet afternoon. Sometimes that works. Usually it produces articles nobody searched for, while the questions customers actually ask go unanswered. Search data removes the guesswork, because Google is already showing you the words real people used.

This guide gives you a repeatable process: find the queries, read them like a customer, group them, and turn the best ones into pages and articles that earn their place.

Where the Ideas Hide: The Queries Report

Open Google Search Console, choose Performance, and look at the Queries tab over the last three months. You will see phrases where your pages appeared, with impressions and clicks for each. Our analytics, tracking and reporting service reviews this list with clients because it is the richest free source of content ideas any business owns.

Look especially for three patterns. Questions phrased as how, what, why and which. Service phrases combined with a town name. And queries with many impressions but very few clicks, which often mean Google already thinks you are relevant, but your page or title is not yet convincing searchers to choose you.

Reading Queries Like a Customer, Not a Marketer

Read each promising query aloud and ask what the person wanted at that moment. Were they comparing options, looking for a price guide, trying to fix a problem, or ready to hire someone nearby? That intent decides the format. A ready-to-hire query needs a clear service page. A learning query needs a helpful article. A comparison query needs honest pros, cons and guidance.

Notice the exact words customers use, including town names and everyday terms rather than industry jargon. If searchers in your area consistently phrase a service differently from your website, your content should meet them in their language. This is keyword research with evidence, and our Search Console beginners guide will help if the tool itself is still new to you.

Sorting Ideas Into Three Useful Groups

  • Improve: Existing pages with good impressions but weak clicks or thin content. Expanding and clarifying these is often the fastest win.
  • Create: Repeated queries you have no page for. Each becomes a new article or, if the intent is to hire, a proper service page.
  • Ignore for now: One-off, irrelevant or out-of-area queries. Not every phrase deserves content, and chasing them dilutes your site.

Write the groups down with the query evidence beside each idea. That simple discipline stops content planning drifting back into opinion, and lets you check next quarter whether the ideas you acted on earned impressions and clicks of their own.

From a Query to a Page That Deserves to Rank

For each chosen idea, plan the page around the searcher's full need, not just the phrase. List the sub-questions someone with that query would ask next, and answer them in clear sections. Use the query's natural words in the title and headings where they fit honestly, link the new page to your related service page, and link older related articles to the new one so Google and readers can find it.

Check Analytics after publishing, as explained in Search Console versus Google Analytics: Search Console will show whether the page starts appearing for its target queries, while Analytics shows whether visitors stay, read and enquire. A page that appears but does not engage needs better content, not more promotion.

A Monthly Planning Habit That Sticks

Set one short session a month. Export or note the top new queries, update your three groups, choose one improvement and one new piece, and record the decisions. Over a year, this builds a library shaped entirely by proven demand in your own market, including seasonal phrases you can prepare for in advance next year.

Local businesses gain an extra advantage here: query data reveals exactly how nearby customers combine services with town names such as Layyah, Chobara or Chowk Azam, so your pages can answer those searches honestly, stating where you are based and which areas you serve.

Let Data Propose, and Judgement Decide

Search data proposes ideas; your judgement still decides. A query might be frequent but outside the work you want, or seasonal in a way that suits a short guide rather than a main page. The goal is not to chase every phrase Google shows you. It is to spend your limited writing time where evidence says customers are already looking.

Frequently Asked Questions

Where exactly do I find search queries for content ideas?

Open Google Search Console, go to Performance, and select the Queries tab. Set a longer period such as the last three months, sort by impressions, and look for questions, service plus town phrases, and topics where you appear but earn few clicks.

What should I do with queries that already bring clicks?

Protect and strengthen them. The pages earning those clicks are assets, so improve them carefully rather than replacing them, and consider a supporting article that links to them. Sudden rewrites of winning pages can do more harm than good.

How many content ideas should I act on each month?

Quality beats quantity. For most small businesses, one or two well-researched articles or page improvements a month, chosen from real query data, build a stronger library than many rushed posts written without evidence.

Can search data help a brand new website with no queries yet?

Early data will be thin, so start with keyword research and customer questions, then switch to query-led planning as Search Console data builds. Within a few months of publishing, real queries usually appear and the process in this guide becomes usable.
