Google Search Console: A Beginner Guide for Website Owners

Search Console is Google talking directly to you about your website: what people searched, which pages appeared, and whether anything is broken. Here is how to read it without feeling overwhelmed.

By Zaheer SEO Expert · 27 October 2026 · Analytics & Reporting

If Google Analytics shows what visitors do after they arrive on your website, Google Search Console shows what happened before the click. It is a free tool where Google reports how your pages appear in search results, which searches brought people to you, and whether Google can properly read and index your pages. For any business that depends on being found, it is essential.

Beginners often open Search Console once, see unfamiliar charts and warnings, and close it again. This guide takes a calmer path: three main areas, in order, and what each one is really telling you.

Setting Up and Verifying Your Website

Before any data appears, Google asks you to prove you own the website. This is called verification, and it can be done through your domain provider, a small file, or by connecting through Google Analytics. Once verified, data starts building over the following days. Make sure the account belongs to you, the business owner, and not only to a developer or agency. Our analytics, tracking and reporting service includes checking that both Analytics and Search Console are set up under the right ownership, because losing access to your own data is a common and avoidable problem.

After verification, the first useful step is submitting your sitemap, which is covered below. Then give the tool some time. Search Console data usually lags by a few days, so yesterday's chart is rarely complete.

The Performance Report: Searches, Clicks and Visibility

Performance is the report most owners should learn first. It shows four main numbers. Clicks are visits from Google search. Impressions count how often your pages appeared in results. Average CTR is the share of impressions that became clicks, and average position is a rough guide to where your pages appeared on average.

Below the chart you can switch between queries, pages, countries and devices. The queries list is especially valuable because it shows the real words people typed. A shop in Layyah might discover that customers search with the town name included far more often than expected. That discovery should shape your page titles and content, as explained in our guide on using search data to plan content.

  • Queries: The searches where your site appeared.
  • Pages: Which of your pages earned clicks and impressions.
  • Devices: Whether searchers mainly used mobile or desktop.
  • Date comparison: This period against the previous one, to see direction.

The Pages Report: Coverage and Indexing Basics

For a page to appear on Google, Google must crawl it and add it to its index. The Pages report, sometimes described as coverage, shows how many of your pages are indexed and how many are not, with a reason for each group. Some reasons are normal, such as old redirected addresses or duplicate pages Google chose to handle differently. Others need action, such as pages blocked by mistake or pages returning errors.

Do not panic at every not-indexed entry. Start with one question: are all my important service and contact pages indexed? Use the URL Inspection tool at the top of Search Console to check a specific page. It tells you whether that page is on Google and lets you request indexing after you publish or fix something important.

Sitemaps: Giving Google a Clean List of Your Pages

A sitemap is a simple file that lists your website's pages. Think of it as handing Google a tidy contents page instead of asking it to guess your structure. In Search Console, open the Sitemaps section, enter your sitemap address, usually ending in sitemap.xml, and submit it. The status should show success, and over time Google reports how many of the listed pages it discovered.

A sitemap only helps if it is accurate. It should list your current, working pages and not old addresses that no longer exist. Whenever your website is rebuilt or pages are renamed, check the sitemap again. A broken or outdated sitemap quietly wastes the tool's main benefit.

Other Sections Worth a Quick Look

Once the basics feel comfortable, glance at a few more areas from time to time. The Links report shows which websites link to you and which of your pages attract the most links. The mobile and page experience sections flag pages that are hard to use on phones. Messages from Google also arrive inside Search Console, so an important warning about your site may appear here before anywhere else.

Remember that Search Console only covers Google search. It does not show social media visits or what people did after clicking. That is why it works as a pair with Analytics, a relationship explained simply in Search Console versus Google Analytics.

A Simple Monthly Search Console Habit

Once a month, open Performance and compare the last three months with the previous three. Note your top queries, any important page that lost clicks, and any indexing warning that mentions your key pages. Check that your sitemap status is still healthy. Write down one action, such as improving a title or expanding a page that is close to earning more clicks.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Google Search Console free?

Yes, Search Console is a free tool from Google. You verify that you own your website, and then Google starts sharing data about how your pages appear in search results and whether any technical problems need attention.

What does impressions mean in Search Console?

An impression means your page appeared somewhere in search results for a query, whether or not anyone clicked it. Impressions show growing visibility, while clicks show how often that visibility turned into an actual visit.

Why is a page not indexed?

Common reasons include the page being new, blocked by settings, having very thin content, or Google simply not having crawled it yet. The Pages report gives a reason for each group of pages, which is the best place to start checking.

Do I need to submit a sitemap more than once?

Usually you submit your sitemap once, and Google returns to it over time as pages change. If you rebuild your website or change your page addresses, check that the sitemap still lists the correct, current pages.
