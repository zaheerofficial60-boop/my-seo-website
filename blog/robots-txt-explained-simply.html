Robots.txt Explained Simply: Basics and Common Mistakes

Robots.txt is a tiny file with great power: used well it guides Google politely, used badly it can lock Google out of your whole website. Here are the basics in plain language.

By Zaheer SEO Expert · 27 October 2026 · Technical SEO

Some of the smallest files on a website have the largest effects. Robots.txt is a plain text file, usually only a few lines long, yet it is the first thing search engine crawlers look for when they arrive at your site. It tells them where they are welcome and where they should not go.

Because the file is so simple, many owners ignore it completely, until a mistake in it quietly blocks important pages from Google. This article explains what robots.txt does, what it does not do, and the errors worth checking for on any business website.

What Robots.txt Does

Imagine a notice on your shop door that says which visitors may enter which rooms. Robots.txt works like that notice, written for crawlers rather than people. It uses simple instructions: a line naming which crawler the rule is for, and lines saying which folders or pages are disallowed. Well-behaved search engines such as Google read the file and respect its rules.

Used sensibly, the file keeps crawlers away from areas that waste their time, such as admin login pages, internal search results, print versions of pages or duplicate folders. It can also point crawlers towards your sitemap, which is why the two topics are closely linked; our sitemap guide explains that side of the partnership.

What Robots.txt Cannot Do

It is just as important to understand the limits. Robots.txt is a request, not a lock. It cannot protect private information, because the file itself is public and anyone can read it. Never use it to hide sensitive pages; anything truly private belongs behind a proper login.

It also cannot fully remove a page from Google. A blocked page that other websites link to may still appear in results as a bare address, because Google knows it exists even though it cannot read it. If a page must stay out of search results, the correct approach is a noindex instruction on the page itself, a distinction covered in our guide on crawlability and indexing.

Common Robots.txt Errors

  • Blocking everything: a rule left over from website development that disallows the entire site, so Google cannot crawl any page at all.
  • Blocking important folders: rules that accidentally cover service pages, product pages or the files a page needs to display properly.
  • Blocking images or scripts the page needs: if Google cannot load the files that build your page, it may judge the page as broken or empty.
  • Using it to hide duplicates: blocking copies instead of fixing the duplication properly, which creates new problems described in our duplicate content guide.
  • Never checking it: the file can be changed by plugins, developers or migrations without the owner ever knowing.

Because the consequences can be severe, reviewing this file carefully is a fixed step in our technical SEO service. One line changed in the wrong place can undo months of good SEO work.

How to Check Your Own File

Checking the basics is easier than most people expect. Type your website address followed by /robots.txt into a browser and the file will usually appear. Read it slowly. If you see a rule that disallows everything, or blocks folders containing your main pages, that is a warning sign. Google Search Console also offers reports and warnings connected to blocked pages.

A safe habitAfter any website redesign, migration or new plugin, re-read your robots.txt file. Many blocking disasters happen quietly during exactly those changes.

Robots.txt rewards care and punishes carelessness, but it is not something to fear. A short, correct file quietly guides crawlers to your best pages and away from the clutter, which is all it was ever meant to do.

A Sensible Robots.txt for a Small Business

For a typical small business website, a healthy robots.txt file is reassuringly boring. It allows crawlers to visit the public pages, steers them away from the administration and login areas, and includes a line pointing to the sitemap so crawlers can find the pages that matter. That is usually all it needs to say. Long, complicated files full of clever rules are rarely necessary for a small site, and every extra rule is another chance to block something important by mistake.

If you inherit a website, or take over one built by someone else, reading this file is one of the cheapest safety checks available to you. Compare what it blocks against what your business actually wants found on Google. If a folder containing services, products or articles appears in the blocked list, ask why before assuming it belongs there. Five careful minutes with this small file can protect the visibility that every other part of your SEO depends upon.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is robots.txt in simple words?

Robots.txt is a small text file on your website that tells search engine crawlers which parts of the site they may visit and which parts to leave alone. It is the first file crawlers usually check when they arrive.

Can robots.txt hide a page completely?

Not reliably. Blocking a page in robots.txt stops crawlers reading it, but the page address can still appear in search results if other sites link to it. To keep a page out of results properly, a noindex instruction is the right tool.

What is the most dangerous robots.txt mistake?

Blocking the whole website by accident is the most serious error. A single misplaced rule can tell Google not to crawl anything, and traffic can fall until the mistake is noticed and fixed.

Does my website need a robots.txt file?

Most websites have one, even a very simple one, because it can guide crawlers away from admin and duplicate areas and point them to the sitemap. It should be set up carefully and checked after any major site change.
