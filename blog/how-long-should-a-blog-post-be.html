How Long Should a Blog Post Be? An Honest Answer

Forget magic word counts. This guide explains how to judge the right length for each article by the question it answers, and why completeness beats padding every time.

By Zaheer SEO Expert · 27 October 2026 · Content Strategy

How long should a blog post be? It is one of the most common questions business owners ask, and the internet answers it with confident numbers that contradict each other. The honest answer is less tidy but far more useful: a post should be as long as it needs to be to answer its question completely, and not one paragraph longer. Length is a result of doing the job properly, not a target to chase for its own sake.

This article explains how to judge the right length for your articles, why both very thin and heavily padded posts fail, and how we approach length inside our content strategy and copywriting service when planning content for local businesses.

Why There Is No Magic Number

Different questions need different amounts of explanation. A post answering what time a market opens needs a few lines. A guide helping a family choose between solar systems needs careful detail about load, batteries, costs factors and maintenance. Forcing both into the same word count would damage both: one becomes padded and annoying, the other thin and unhelpful.

Search engines do not reward length itself; they reward satisfied readers. A long article that fully answers a complex question often performs well because complete answers attract readers, links and time on the page. A long article that repeats itself performs poorly because readers leave. The length was never the active ingredient; the usefulness was.

The Real Problem With Short, Thin Posts

Short posts fail when they are thin, not when they are short. A thin post states the obvious, repeats the title in different words and ends before the reader has learned anything they could act on. Visitors leave with their question unanswered, and Google learns that the page does not satisfy the search. Many small business blogs are full of such posts, published quickly to fill a schedule, and they quietly achieve nothing.

If a topic genuinely needs only a brief answer, publish it briefly but completely, perhaps as part of a questions section on a service page. Save standalone articles for questions with enough substance to deserve them, as discussed in our guide to using customer questions as content.

The Equally Real Problem With Padded Posts

Padding is the opposite failure: stretching a simple answer with repeated points, general filler and paragraphs that exist only to reach a number. Readers can feel padding within seconds, and their trust drains quickly. A padded article also buries its own answer, so even the good information inside it stops working. Writing more is only valuable when each addition answers a real follow-up question or adds a useful example, step or warning.

A practical test while editing: for each paragraph, ask what the reader knows after it that they did not know before. If the answer is nothing, cut the paragraph, however proud of it you are. Articles usually get better, and clearer, in this kind of editing.

A Practical Way to Find the Right Length

Before writing, list the sub-questions a reader would naturally ask about your topic: the main answer, how it works, what it depends on, common mistakes, costs factors where relevant, and what to do next. Write one clear section per sub-question. When every listed question has been answered honestly, the article is finished, whether that took a compact piece or a long guide. This method, part of a planned blog content plan, produces natural lengths that vary by topic, which is exactly what a healthy blog looks like.

  • Simple, single-fact questions: answer briefly and completely.
  • How-to topics: long enough for every step and warning.
  • Comparisons: long enough to treat each option fairly.
  • Buying guides: long enough for factors, examples and next steps.
  • Local guides: long enough to cover real areas and situations.

What About the Articles on This Website?

On this site we generally aim for substantial, complete articles on business topics, because our readers are owners making real decisions and the questions deserve full answers. Most guides here run to a length that allows examples and follow-up questions to be covered properly. But the aim is always completeness first. If a future topic can be answered well in fewer words, we will use fewer words, and if one needs more room, it will get it.

Consistency of quality matters more than consistency of length. Readers return to blogs that respect their intelligence and their time, in whatever number of paragraphs that requires.

Length, Effort and Your Schedule

For a busy owner, the length question is often really a time question. Fewer, complete articles published steadily will outperform many rushed thin ones, and they are easier to maintain. If a big guide feels overwhelming, write it in stages across the week, or split a broad topic into a small series of complete articles that link to each other. Each piece must still stand on its own and answer its own question fully.

So, how long should your next blog post be? Long enough that a reader closes it with their question answered, their next step clear, and no reason to search again. Write to that standard, count words afterwards if you like, and you will rarely go wrong.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is there an ideal word count for SEO?

No single number works for every topic. Pages perform well when they answer their question completely and clearly. Some topics need a short piece; guides and comparisons usually need much more. Completeness matters, not the count itself.

Are longer articles always better for rankings?

No. Longer articles only help when the extra length adds real answers, examples and steps. Padded articles that repeat points frustrate readers and can perform worse than a shorter, complete answer.

What is wrong with very short blog posts?

Nothing, if the question is simple and fully answered. The problem is thin posts that promise an answer and deliver only a few obvious lines. Those leave readers unsatisfied and rarely earn good positions on Google.

How do I know when my article is finished?

List the follow-up questions a reader would ask about the topic before you write. When each has been answered honestly and the next step is clear, the article is complete, whatever its final length.
