How to Read an SEO Report, Section by Section

A good SEO report should leave you clearer, not more confused. This guide walks through a typical monthly report in order, so you know what each section means and what questions to ask.

By Zaheer SEO Expert · 27 October 2026 · Analytics & Reporting

Every month, many business owners receive a document full of charts, percentages and unfamiliar words, glance at the first page, and file it away none the wiser. That is a waste. An SEO report is meant to answer three simple questions: what was done, what changed as a result, and what happens next. If your report does not answer those clearly, the problem is the report, not you.

This guide goes through a typical monthly report section by section, in the order you will usually meet them. Keep a pen nearby and note anything you cannot explain in your own words afterwards. Those notes become your questions for the next conversation.

Start With the Summary at the Top

Most honest reports open with a short summary: the month in a few lines. It should say plainly whether visibility and enquiries rose, fell or stayed steady, and mention the main reason, such as new pages published, a seasonal dip, or a technical fix. Our analytics, tracking and reporting service treats this summary as the most important paragraph in the whole document, because many owners only have time to read one page.

Compare the summary with the sections that follow. If the summary claims a great month but the enquiry numbers inside are flat, ask why. A summary should reflect the evidence in the report, not replace it.

The Work Completed Section

Next usually comes a list of work done: pages improved, articles published, technical issues fixed, listings updated. Read this against last month's promised next steps. Did the planned work actually happen? Vague lines such as SEO activities performed tell you nothing. Specific lines, such as three service pages updated and a new article published, can be checked on your own website in a minute.

This section matters because SEO is a service you pay for over time. You are entitled to a clear record of effort, just as you would expect from any other supplier. Keep past reports together so you can see the pattern of work across several months, not just one.

Visibility: Impressions, Clicks and Positions

The visibility section normally comes from Google Search Console. Impressions show how often your pages appeared in search results, clicks show how many visits followed, and average position gives a rough sense of where you appeared. Rising impressions with steady clicks often mean you are showing for more searches but your titles need work. Rising clicks are the healthier sign.

Do not judge this section by a single keyword. Individual positions move up and down daily and differ by town and device. Judge the overall direction across your important pages, the same approach explained in SEO numbers that actually matter.

Traffic: Who Visited and What They Did

Traffic figures usually come from Google Analytics. Look for visits from organic search, the pages people viewed most, and how many used a mobile phone. A healthy pattern is more search visits landing on useful service and article pages, not only the homepage. If one article brings many visits, that topic deserves more attention and a clearer path to contacting you.

Be careful with time-on-page and similar measures. They can hint at interest, but they are rough guides, not proof. The traffic section earns its place when it connects to the next section: actions.

Enquiries: Calls, Messages and Forms

For a local business, this is the heart of the report. How many people called from your website or Google Business Profile, asked for directions, sent a message or submitted a form? These numbers connect SEO to real business. Our guide on measuring local SEO success shows how to judge these actions fairly, including why a quiet month after a busy season is not automatically a failure.

Check that the report separates real enquiries from general visits. A report that celebrates traffic while hiding enquiry numbers is telling you the less important half of the story.

Next Steps and Questions You Should Ask

A good report ends with next steps: what will be done next month and why. Each step should link to something the report found, such as improving a page with many impressions but few clicks. If next steps look identical every month, ask what is driving the plan.

  • What changed most this month, and why?
  • Which page brought the most enquiries?
  • What did not work, and what will change?
  • What do you need from me to do next month well?

Frequently Asked Questions

How often should I receive an SEO report?

Monthly reporting suits most small businesses. SEO changes take time to show in search results, so a monthly view with a comparison to the previous period gives a fairer picture than daily or weekly snapshots.

What if I do not understand a term in my report?

Ask for a plain explanation. A trustworthy provider should be happy to explain any term, chart or number in simple words. If a report relies on jargon to impress you, that is a warning sign, not a sign of expertise.

Should my report promise rankings for specific keywords?

Be careful with fixed ranking promises. Search results change with location, device and competition, and no honest provider can guarantee an exact position. Look instead for overall growth in visibility, clicks and enquiries over time.

What is the most important section of an SEO report?

For most business owners, the enquiries section matters most: calls, messages and form submissions linked to your website and Google profile. Visibility and traffic explain how those enquiries happened, but enquiries pay the bills.
