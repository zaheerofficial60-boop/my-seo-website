Title Tag Optimization: Writing Titles That Rank and Get Clicks

Your title tag is the first thing people see on Google and one of the strongest signals about your page. This guide shows how to write titles that are clear, honest and worth clicking.

By Zaheer SEO Expert · 27 October 2026 · On-Page SEO

Before anyone reads your website, they read your title. In Google results, the title tag becomes the blue clickable headline. It has two jobs at once: tell Google what the page is about, and give a searching customer a reason to choose your result over the others on the page.

Many business websites waste this space. Titles say Home, Services, or just the business name repeated on every page. Those titles tell Google almost nothing and give customers no reason to click. Fixing titles is one of the simplest parts of our on-page SEO service, and often one of the most rewarding.

What Makes a Good Title Tag?

A good title names the topic or service first, in the words a customer would search for. It is specific rather than general, and it matches what the page actually delivers. If your title promises one thing and the page gives another, visitors leave quickly and trust is lost.

A simple pattern works for most service pages: Service or Topic, then a helpful detail or location, then your business name. For an article, the pattern is the same: main topic first, business name last. This keeps the most important words at the front, where they are least likely to be cut off on a mobile screen.

  • Put the main keyword or topic near the beginning.
  • Write one clear idea per title, not a list of services.
  • Keep it readable as a normal phrase, not a string of keywords.
  • Make every page title unique across your website.

Weak Titles and Stronger Alternatives

Consider a page about plumbing repairs. A weak title is just Home or Welcome to Our Website. It could belong to any business in any town. A stronger title names the service directly, such as Plumbing Repairs and the kind of help offered. The stronger version helps Google match the page to the right searches and helps the customer decide in seconds.

The same logic applies to articles. A title like Tips is meaningless on its own. A title that names the exact problem the article solves tells the reader immediately whether to click. When you review your own site, cover the page content and read only the titles. If you cannot tell what each page does from its title alone, the titles need work.

Length, Accuracy and Honesty

Very long titles get cut off in results, especially on phones, so keep yours concise. Short is not automatically better, though. A title that is too short often becomes vague. Aim for a complete, natural phrase that fits comfortably, and check how it looks rather than chasing an exact character count.

Honesty matters as much as length. Do not add words like cheapest, guaranteed or best unless the page genuinely supports them and you can stand behind the claim. Clicks won through exaggeration turn into quick exits, and quick exits do not build a business. A clear, truthful title paired with a helpful page is the combination that lasts. It also works hand in hand with your description, which our guide on meta descriptions that get clicks covers next.

Keywords in Titles Without Stuffing

Using your main keyword once, naturally, is enough. Repeating it two or three times in one title looks desperate and reads badly. Google understands related words and the overall topic of the page, so you do not need to force every variation into the title.

Think about the search behind the title. A customer searching for a service usually includes the service name and sometimes the town. Including the town makes sense for a local service page, but adding five town names to one title does not. Each location deserves its own honest page if you serve it, not a crowded title.

How Titles Work With Headings

Your title tag and your main heading, the H1, should agree with each other. They do not need to be identical word for word, but a visitor who clicks a title and lands on a page with a completely different heading feels misled. Keeping them closely related reassures the visitor they are in the right place.

This connection is part of overall page structure. If your headings feel messy, read our guide to heading structure for SEO after fixing your titles, because the two elements support each other on every page.

A Quick Routine for Reviewing All Your Titles

List every page on your site with its current title in a simple document or spreadsheet. Then check each one against four questions: Is it unique? Does it name the topic clearly? Would a customer understand it without seeing the page? Is it honest about what the page offers? Rewrite the failures first, starting with your most important service pages.

After changing titles, give Google time to update its results and watch which pages start receiving more visits. Title work is not glamorous, but it is foundational. Get it right and every other improvement on the page has a better chance of being seen.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long should a title tag be?

Keep it short enough to display fully in search results, usually around 50 to 60 characters. More important than an exact number is clarity: the main topic should appear early and the wording should read naturally.

Should every page have a different title tag?

Yes. Each page covers a different topic or service, so each title should reflect that. Duplicate titles confuse Google and make it harder for customers to tell your pages apart in search results.

Should I put my business name in every title?

It often helps to add your business name at the end, especially for service pages, because it builds recognition. Put the service or topic first so the most important words are not cut off on small screens.

Why did Google show a different title than the one I wrote?

Google sometimes rewrites titles when it thinks another line from the page matches the search better. Clear, accurate titles that match the page heading and content are less likely to be rewritten.
