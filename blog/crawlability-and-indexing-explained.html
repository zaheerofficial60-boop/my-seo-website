Crawlability and Indexing Explained: How Google Finds Your Pages

Before a page can rank, Google must first find it, read it and store it. This guide explains crawling and indexing in plain language, and the blockers that quietly keep pages out of search.

By Zaheer SEO Expert · 27 October 2026 · Technical SEO

Many business owners are surprised to learn that simply publishing a page does not put it on Google. Between publishing and appearing in search results, a quiet process takes place in stages. If any stage fails, the page stays invisible, no matter how well it is written. Understanding those stages is one of the most useful things an owner can learn about SEO.

The two key words are crawling and indexing. They sound technical, but the ideas behind them are everyday ones, similar to how a librarian discovers new books and shelves them so readers can find them.

Step One: Crawling, When Google Visits Your Pages

Crawling is the discovery stage. Google uses automated programs, often called crawlers or bots, that travel from link to link across the web. When a crawler reaches your website, it reads your pages, notes the links on them, and follows those links to find more pages. A page that no other page links to, and that appears in no sitemap, can be very hard for a crawler to discover at all.

Crawlability simply means how easily these crawlers can move around your site. Broken links, pages that return errors, very slow loading and settings that block crawlers all reduce crawlability. Think of it as the condition of the roads leading to and through your shop: if the roads are blocked, the delivery vans never arrive.

Step Two: Indexing, When Google Stores Your Pages

After reading a page, Google decides whether to store it in its index, the enormous library it searches when someone types a query. Only indexed pages can appear in results. Google may choose not to index a page if it looks like a copy of another page, if it contains very little useful content, or if the page itself carries an instruction asking not to be indexed.

This is why indexing problems are so frustrating: the page looks perfectly normal to you in a browser, yet it is absent from Google's library. Our technical SEO service includes checking exactly which pages are indexed and which are missing, because you cannot fix a problem you cannot see.

Common Blockers That Keep Pages Out of Google

  • A noindex instruction: a small setting on a page that tells Google not to store it, sometimes left on by accident after a website launch.
  • A blocking robots.txt rule: a file that can stop crawlers from visiting parts of a site, explained in our robots.txt guide.
  • No internal links: a page that nothing links to is easy for crawlers to miss entirely.
  • Server errors: pages that fail to load when the crawler visits cannot be read or stored.
  • Duplicate versions: Google may index one version and skip near-identical copies.

Two helpful tools support this whole process. An XML sitemap lists the pages you want Google to know about, as explained in our sitemap guide, and Google Search Console shows you reports on which pages are indexed and which have problems.

How to Help Google Crawl and Index Your Site

The good news is that helping Google is mostly common sense. Link every important page from somewhere sensible, such as your menu, your homepage or a related article. Keep page addresses clear and stable instead of changing them often. Fix broken links when pages move, using proper redirects so crawlers and customers land in the right place. Keep your sitemap up to date, and make sure your site loads reliably so visits from crawlers succeed.

A quick indexing checkSearch Google using your website name plus a service you offer. Then check Search Console's page report if you have access. Important pages that never appear anywhere deserve a closer look.

Crawling and indexing are the doorway to everything else in SEO. Content, keywords and links only matter for pages Google has actually stored. Keeping that doorway open is quiet, unglamorous work, but it protects all the effort you put into your website.

New Pages Need a Little Patience

Owners sometimes worry when a page published yesterday does not appear in Google today. That delay is normal. Crawlers visit websites on their own schedule, and a small site may wait days or longer before a new page is discovered, read and added to the index. Publishing the page, linking it from a sensible place, including it in the sitemap and then waiting calmly is usually the correct response, rather than repeatedly deleting and republishing it, which only restarts the process.

Patience has limits, though. If an important page is still missing after a reasonable wait, that is the moment to investigate methodically: check for blocking settings, confirm other pages link to it, and look at the reports in Search Console. Most indexing mysteries turn out to have ordinary explanations, and working through them in order almost always reveals which stage of the journey the page is stuck at.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between crawling and indexing?

Crawling is when Google visits and reads a page. Indexing is when Google stores that page in its index so it can appear in search results. A page must usually be crawled before it can be indexed and shown.

Why is my page not showing on Google?

Common reasons include the page being blocked by a setting, having no links pointing to it, being very new, or being too similar to another page. Checking Google Search Console usually reveals which reason applies to your page.

How does Google find new pages on my website?

Google mainly follows links from pages it already knows and reads your XML sitemap, which lists the pages you want indexed. Linking new pages from relevant existing pages helps Google discover them faster.

Can I force Google to index a page immediately?

You can request indexing in Google Search Console, which asks Google to visit the page, but Google still decides if and when to include it. Good internal links and a healthy site make indexing more reliable than repeated requests.
