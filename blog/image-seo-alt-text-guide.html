Image SEO: File Names, Alt Text and Compression Explained

Images make pages welcoming and clear, but unmanaged images slow sites down and tell Google nothing. This guide covers the three habits that fix that: naming, describing and compressing.

By Zaheer SEO Expert · 27 October 2026 · On-Page SEO

People understand images instantly, but search engines do not see them the way we do. Google needs words around and behind an image to understand what it shows. At the same time, visitors on mobile phones need images that load quickly. Image SEO balances both needs, and it is a regular part of our on-page SEO service.

The good news is that image SEO is mostly a routine. Once you build the habit of renaming, describing and compressing every image before upload, the extra work per image is less than a minute, and your whole site benefits.

File Names: The First Clue You Give Google

Most cameras and phones save photos with names like IMG_5821 or DSC0093. Those names tell Google nothing. Renaming the file before upload to describe the picture, using words separated by hyphens, gives a small but real clue about the content.

Keep names short and accurate. A photo of your team reviewing a website might become team-reviewing-website.jpg. A shopfront photo might become clothing-shop-layyah.jpg if that is genuinely what it shows. Do not add keywords that do not match the picture. Accuracy beats cleverness, and a misleading name helps no one.

Alt Text: Describing Images for People and Search

Alt text is a short description attached to an image in the page code. Its first purpose is accessibility: screen readers read it aloud for visitors who cannot see the image. Its second purpose is context for Google. Both purposes are served by the same thing, an honest description.

Write alt text as a plain sentence fragment describing the subject. Mention who or what is in the image and what is happening. You do not need to start with image of or photo of, because that is already understood. Include a keyword only when it fits the description naturally, for example if the page topic is genuinely visible in the picture.

Every meaningful image deserves its own alt text rather than the same sentence copied across the site. Copied alt text is a missed signal and a poor experience for anyone relying on it. If an image adds no information and is only decoration, it is better left with empty alt text so it is skipped gracefully.

Compression and Size: Keeping Pages Fast

A beautiful image that takes forever to load is a liability. Many business sites upload original phone photos that are far larger than the space they fill on screen. The visitor pays for that size with waiting time, especially on mobile data, and many will leave before the page appears.

The fix has three steps. First, resize the image to roughly the dimensions it will be displayed at. Second, compress it with a free tool or your website editor so the file becomes much smaller while still looking clear. Third, choose a sensible format: JPG for most photographs, PNG when you need transparency or crisp text in a graphic. Placement and Context Around Images

Where an image sits matters too. Place images near the text they illustrate, not gathered randomly at the bottom. The nearby headings and paragraphs give Google additional context, and the reader gets a natural break that makes long pages easier to follow.

Captions are optional, but a short caption can help when an image needs explanation, such as a before and after example or a specific product view. Keep captions truthful and brief, like the rest of your page writing.

Images in Google Image Search and Local Trust

Some customers discover businesses through image results, especially for products, food, interiors and completed work. Well named and described images have a better chance of appearing there. More importantly for local businesses, real photos of your shop, team and work build trust in a way generic decoration cannot.

Use your own photos where possible. A genuine photo of your premises in Layyah tells a local customer more than a polished stock image of a place they will never visit. Combine real photos with the naming and alt text habits in this guide, and your images start working for you instead of just filling space. For the wider page picture, see our on-page SEO checklist.

A Simple Routine for Every New Image

Before uploading any image, run through four checks: rename the file descriptively, resize it to a sensible display size, compress it, and write unique alt text after placing it. Add this routine to whoever publishes on your site, so quality does not depend on one person's memory.

For existing sites, start with the images on your busiest pages. Fix those first, then work through older articles over time. Image SEO is steady, unglamorous maintenance, and that is exactly why it separates careful websites from careless ones.

Frequently Asked Questions

What should I write in alt text?

Describe the image briefly and honestly as if explaining it to someone who cannot see it. Mention the main subject and action. Keep it natural and avoid stuffing keywords into every description.

Does the image file name really matter?

It helps a little and costs nothing. A descriptive name like team-reviewing-website.jpg gives Google context that IMG_2041.jpg does not. Rename files before uploading as part of your normal routine.

How do I make images load faster?

Resize images to the size they will actually be shown, compress them before uploading, and use a common format like JPG for photos. Avoid uploading huge original camera files directly to your pages.

Should decorative images have alt text?

Purely decorative images can have empty alt text so screen readers skip them. Important content images should always have a meaningful description because they carry information for the reader.
