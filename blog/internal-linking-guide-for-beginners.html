Internal Linking Guide for Beginners: Connect Your Pages Properly

Internal links are the paths between your own pages. This beginner guide explains why they matter and how to add them in a way that helps real visitors, not just search engines.

By Zaheer SEO Expert · 27 October 2026 · On-Page SEO

An internal link is simply a link from one page of your website to another page on the same website. Your menu contains internal links, but this guide is about the more valuable kind: links inside your paragraphs, placed where a reader naturally wants more detail or a next step.

Beginners often overlook these links because each one seems small. Together, they form the structure of your whole site. Our on-page SEO service always includes an internal linking review, because disconnected pages waste both content and opportunity.

Why Internal Links Matter for Visitors

A good internal link arrives at the exact moment a reader thinks, I want to know more about that. It saves them searching your site manually and keeps them moving toward an answer or a decision. Without these links, every page is a dead end, and visitors must start again from the menu or from Google.

Think of your website as a shop. The homepage is the entrance, service pages are the aisles, and articles are the helpful staff explaining options. Internal links are the signs that guide a customer from one aisle to the next. Good signs do not shout. They appear where they are useful and use clear words.

Why Internal Links Matter for Google

Google discovers and understands pages partly by following links. A page that many relevant pages link to looks important. A page with no incoming links, sometimes called an orphan page, can be missed entirely or treated as unimportant, however good its content is.

Links also pass context. The words used in a link, called anchor text, hint at what the destination page covers. When you link with descriptive words to a related guide, you help Google connect the topics correctly. This is one reason our on-page SEO checklist treats linking as a page level habit, not an afterthought.

Where to Place Internal Links Naturally

The easiest place is inside a sentence that already mentions the related topic. If an article about headings mentions titles, link the title guide there. If a service page mentions an audit as a first step, link to the audit page in that sentence. The link should feel like a natural part of the writing, because for the reader, it is.

  • Link from articles to the most relevant service page.
  • Link between articles that cover neighbouring topics.
  • Link from a general page down to a more specific page.
  • Link back from a detailed page to the main overview page.
  • Add a link to a new page from at least one older, relevant page.

Avoid placing links in a long block at the bottom with no context, or turning every second sentence into a link. Both patterns annoy readers and dilute the value of each link.

Writing Anchor Text That Helps

Anchor text should describe the destination honestly and briefly. The name of a service, the title of a guide, or a short phrase naming the topic all work well. Vague phrases like click here or read more tell the reader nothing until they hover or tap, and they tell Google nothing at all.

Do not use the exact same keyword phrase for every link to a page across your whole site. Natural variation is healthier: sometimes the full guide title, sometimes a shorter form, sometimes a related phrase inside a sentence. If the link reads smoothly to a human, it is usually right for Google too.

A Simple Linking Plan for a Small Website

Start by listing your pages in three groups: your main service pages, your location or audience pages, and your articles. Every article should link to at least one service page and one related article. Every service page should link to a helpful article or a next step, such as an audit or contact page.

Then check for orphans. Any page that nothing links to needs at least one sensible incoming link from a related page. Finally, click through your own links as a visitor would. Broken links and links to the wrong page damage trust quickly, so fix them as soon as you find them.

Common Internal Linking Mistakes

Linking only to the homepage is a frequent mistake. The homepage is general, while the reader usually needs the specific service or answer. Another mistake is linking to irrelevant pages just to add links. An irrelevant link confuses the reader about why it is there and weakens the signal of your good links.

Also avoid hiding important pages several clicks deep with no shortcuts from popular pages. If a page matters to your business, it should be reachable in a couple of clicks from somewhere visible. Simple structure beats clever structure every time.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many internal links should a page have?

There is no magic number. Add links where they genuinely help the reader reach a related page. A typical article might link to a few relevant pages. Quality and relevance matter far more than quantity.

What words should I use for a link?

Use descriptive words that tell the reader what the next page covers, such as the name of a service or guide. Avoid vague text like click here, because it gives neither readers nor Google useful information.

Do internal links help new pages get found?

Yes. Linking to a new page from an existing relevant page gives Google a path to discover it and helps visitors find it too. A page with no links pointing to it can easily be overlooked.

Should I link to the same page many times in one article?

Usually once is enough in the main text. Repeating the same link many times looks forced and does not add help for the reader. Spread your links across the different pages that are truly relevant.
