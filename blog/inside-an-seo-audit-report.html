Inside an SEO Audit Report: Section by Section

A walk through what a good SEO audit report actually contains, so you know what to expect and how to use the findings with confidence.

By Zaheer SEO Expert · 27 October 2026 · SEO Audits

If you have never seen an SEO audit report, it can sound mysterious or overly technical. In reality, a good report is simply an organised document that explains the health of your website in plain language, section by section, with clear priorities.

This article walks through what a proper audit report contains, so you know what to expect, what to look for, and what questions to ask when you receive one.

The Summary: Findings at a Glance

A good report opens with a short summary. It states the overall condition of the site, the most serious problems found, and the biggest opportunities. This section is written for the business owner, not for technicians, and it should be understandable on a first reading.

If a report jumps straight into raw data with no summary, that is a warning sign about the report itself. The whole point is clarity. You should finish the first page knowing what matters most.

Technical Health Section

The next section covers the technical foundations: whether Google can crawl and index your pages, how fast they load, how they work on mobile phones, and whether there are broken links, duplicate pages, or security issues.

Each finding should name the affected pages and explain why it matters in ordinary words. For example, instead of only saying a technical term, the report should explain that certain pages cannot be found by Google and therefore cannot bring visitors. This is the standard followed in our SEO audits and competitor analysis service.

On-Page and Content Section

This section reviews titles, headings, and the quality of your content. It flags missing or duplicated titles, thin pages, topics that are missing entirely, and pages that compete with each other by covering the same subject in the same way.

  • Pages with missing, duplicate, or unclear titles
  • Thin pages that need expanding or merging
  • Important services with no dedicated page
  • Internal links that are missing or confusing
  • Images without descriptions or with very large files

It should also note what is already working. An honest report is balanced, because knowing your strengths helps you protect them while fixing weaknesses. For background, our on-page SEO checklist shows the kind of items this section is built from.

Links, Listings, and Local Presence

A complete report looks beyond your own pages. It reviews the links pointing to your site for quality, checks your business details across listings for consistency, and examines your Google Business Profile: category, hours, photos, services, and reviews.

Problems here are often simple but valuable: an old phone number on a directory, a profile with no photos, or important services missing from the profile. These are the kinds of fixes owners can often make quickly once they are pointed out.

Competitor Snapshot and Priorities

Many reports include a short competitor comparison, showing how your visibility compares with the businesses appearing above you and what they do differently. This context stops the report being a list of faults in a vacuum.

Most importantly, the report ends with priorities. Findings are ordered: urgent fixes first, important improvements next, and longer-term ideas last, ideally with a note on how difficult each fix is. Without priorities, a long report overwhelms instead of helping.

How to Use Your Report

Read the summary first, then the priorities. Ask questions about anything unclear; a good provider welcomes them. Then work through fixes in order, ticking them off, and keep the report to compare against the next review.

Beware of reports that are only an automated score with no explanation, or that promise specific rankings. A real report, like the process described in what an SEO audit is, gives you understanding and a plan, not magic numbers.

Red Flags to Watch for in a Report

Just as a good report has clear signs, a poor one does too. Be cautious if a report is mostly screenshots from an automated tool with no explanation written for your business, if it lists hundreds of problems with no order of importance, or if it promises exact ranking positions as part of the findings. Those are signs of a generic product, not a real examination of your site.

Also watch for findings you cannot verify. A trustworthy report points to specific pages and specific issues that you can check yourself in a browser or in Search Console. If you ask where a problem can be seen and the answer is vague, the finding itself deserves doubt.

Finally, a good report should be honest about what is already healthy. A document that declares everything terrible is often preparing you for a large sale rather than describing your website fairly.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should an audit report be written in plain language?

Yes. Technical terms may appear, but each finding should be explained in ordinary words, with the affected pages named and the reason it matters made clear to the business owner.

Is a single automated score the same as an audit report?

No. A score is one rough indicator. A real report examines your site in sections, explains each problem, compares competitors where useful, and orders the fixes by priority.

What if I disagree with something in the report?

Ask the provider to show you the evidence for that finding. Good audits are based on checkable facts about your pages, links, and listings, and questions should always be welcome.

How long should an audit report be?

Length depends on the size of the site. What matters is not page count but clarity: a summary, organised sections, and a prioritised action list you can actually work through.
