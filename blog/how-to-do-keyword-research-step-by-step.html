KEYWORD RESEARCH

How to Do Keyword Research Step by Step: A Practical Process

No expensive tools and no jargon. This practical walkthrough shows you how to go from a blank page to an organised keyword list your website can actually use.

By Zaheer SEO Expert · 27 October 2026 · Keyword Research

Keyword research does not need expensive software or a marketing degree. It needs a clear process, a notebook or spreadsheet, and honest attention to how your customers speak. This step-by-step guide takes you from a blank page to an organised keyword list you can actually use on your website. Work through the steps in order the first time. Later, you can repeat the same process whenever you add a service or a new page.

Before you begin, set aside a quiet hour and open a simple spreadsheet with columns for the keyword, what the searcher wants, and which page it belongs to. That sheet becomes the working heart of your SEO. If the idea of keywords is still new to you, read what keyword research is first, then come back here.

Step 1: List Your Services in Customer Language

Write down everything you sell or do, one item per line. Then rewrite each item the way a customer would say it. Customers rarely use technical or trade names. They describe the problem they have or the result they want. Ask family members, staff, and regular customers how they would search for each service. Their answers are often different from yours, and their answers are the ones that matter, because they are the searchers.

Do not judge the list yet. At this stage, more ideas are better. Include spelling variations, Urdu-influenced phrasing written in English letters where people commonly search that way, and phrases that include your town or nearby areas if you serve local customers.

Step 2: Expand the List With Google Suggestions

Take each seed phrase to Google. Type it slowly and note the suggestions Google offers before you press enter. These autocomplete suggestions come from real searches, so each one is a small piece of evidence about demand. Then run the search and scroll to the related searches near the bottom of the results page. Add the relevant ones to your sheet.

Also notice the questions Google shows in the results. Those questions make excellent blog topics and FAQ entries, because they are literally the questions your future customers are asking. Repeat this for every service on your list. Your sheet will grow quickly, and that is fine.

Step 3: Check What Competitors Target

Search for your main services and look at the businesses that appear on the first page, especially businesses similar in size to yours. Open their pages and read their titles and headings. You are not copying their text. You are learning which phrases the market uses and which topics Google currently rewards. Note any useful phrase you had missed and add it to your list.

Step 4: Understand the Intent Behind Each Keyword

For every keyword, ask one question: what does this person want right now? Some searchers want information and are comparing options. Some are ready to contact a business. Some are looking for one specific shop or brand. Write the intent in your spreadsheet next to the keyword. A phrase with buying intent belongs on a service page. A question belongs in a blog post or FAQ. Mixing these up is a common reason pages fail, and our guide to search intent explained simply covers this in depth.

Step 5: Judge Demand and Difficulty Honestly

You will not have perfect numbers without paid tools, and you do not need them. Use common sense. A phrase that Google suggests, competitors target, and customers mention clearly has demand. Then ask whether a business like yours can realistically compete. Very general phrases are dominated by large brands and directories. Specific phrases that combine a service, a need, and often a place are far more reachable. A structured keyword research and strategy service follows this same logic, just with more data behind each decision.

  • Keep phrases customers actually use and mention.
  • Keep phrases your direct competitors rank for.
  • Prefer specific phrases over vague, general ones.
  • Remove phrases for services you do not offer.

Step 6: Group Keywords and Assign Them to Pages

Now organise. Group phrases that mean the same thing and would be answered by the same page. Choose one main keyword for each group and a few supporting phrases. Then assign each group to one page: an existing page if you have one, or a new page you plan to create. One group per page keeps your site clear and stops your own pages competing against each other. This organising stage is called keyword mapping, and it turns a messy list into a real plan for your website.

Step 7: Write, Publish, and Review

Finally, use the list. Write each page around its assigned keyword group, in natural language, answering the searcher fully. After publishing, watch Google Search Console over the following weeks to see which phrases your pages actually appear for. You will often discover new phrases real visitors used, which you can add back into your sheet. Keyword research is a loop, not a single event, and each round makes your website stronger and more closely matched to your customers.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does keyword research take for a small website?

A first useful pass usually takes a few focused hours: listing services, expanding with Google suggestions, checking intent, and grouping phrases into a simple spreadsheet plan.

Do I need paid tools to do keyword research?

No. Google suggestions, related searches, competitor pages, customer questions, and Search Console give a small business enough information to build a solid keyword list.

How many keywords should each page target?

Each page should focus on one main keyword and a small group of closely related phrases that mean the same thing, rather than many unrelated keywords.

What do I do with the keyword list after research?

Assign each keyword group to one page, write or improve those pages in natural language, then review Search Console regularly and refresh the list as you learn.
