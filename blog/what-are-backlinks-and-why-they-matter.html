What Are Backlinks and Why Do They Matter for Your Website?

Backlinks sound technical, but the idea is simple: they are recommendations from other websites. This guide explains what they are, why Google trusts them, and why one good link beats a pile of poor ones.

By Zaheer SEO Expert · 27 October 2026 · Off-Page SEO

If you have read anything about SEO, you have probably met the word backlink and a lot of confusing talk around it. This article strips away the jargon. A backlink is simply a link on someone else's website that points to a page on yours. That is all it is. The interesting part is why such a simple thing matters so much to Google, and why some backlinks help while others are worthless or even harmful.

Imagine two electricians in the same town. Both have tidy websites. One is recommended on the local chamber page, mentioned by suppliers, and listed on trusted directories. The other is mentioned nowhere. If you were a stranger choosing between them, the recommendations would sway you. Google works in a similar way, using backlinks as recommendations it can count and judge.

A Simple Way to Picture a Backlink

Every link on the web was placed by someone who thought the page at the other end was worth visiting. When a website links to your page, its owner is effectively saying, this is worth your time. Google treats that as a vote. One vote from a respected, relevant website can count for more than many votes from unknown sites with no connection to your trade.

Backlinks also help Google find your pages in the first place. Google's software follows links across the web, so a new page that is linked from somewhere is usually discovered faster than a page that nothing points to. For a deeper look at how links fit into a wider plan, our off-page SEO and link building service page explains the approach we take.

Not All Backlinks Are Equal

Newcomers often count links as if they were coins, assuming more is always better. Google is more like a careful referee. It asks where the link comes from, whether the site is relevant to yours, and whether the link looks natural. A link from a respected local news site or a trade supplier carries real weight. A link from a random page full of unrelated links carries almost none.

  • Relevance: a link from a site in a related field or your local area makes sense.
  • Trust: established sites with real visitors pass more confidence.
  • Placement: a link inside a helpful article is worth more than one buried in a footer.
  • Anchor text: the clickable words should read naturally, not repeat the same keyword everywhere.

Why Backlinks Influence Rankings

Google's job is to show results people can rely on. Content alone can be copied or exaggerated, but a pattern of genuine recommendations from independent websites is harder to fake. When several good sites link to your guide or your service page, Google gains confidence that the page deserves to be seen. That confidence is one of the reasons established competitors can be hard to overtake even with better written pages.

Backlinks are not the only ranking factor, and they cannot rescue a poor website. Slow pages, thin content and a confusing layout will still hold you back. Think of backlinks as reputation, and your website as the actual quality of your work. You need both: a good service, and other people willing to say so.

Links You Earn Versus Links You Buy

There is a big difference between earning a link and buying one. Earned links happen because you published something useful, helped a customer, joined a local event, or wrote for another site. Bought links are placed purely for money, usually on sites built only to sell links. Google's guidelines treat paid links that pass ranking value as a violation, and sites caught using them can lose visibility.

Our article on link building mistakes to avoid covers the common traps in detail. The short version is simple: if a link offer sounds too cheap and too easy, the links are almost certainly worthless, and they may drag your site down with them.

How Many Backlinks Do You Need?

There is no magic number, and anyone who names an exact figure is guessing. What matters is how your link profile compares with the businesses competing for the same searches, and whether your links look natural and relevant. A small local business may need only a modest number of good local and trade links. A business competing nationally faces a much harder task.

A healthy approach is to grow links steadily as your business does real things worth mentioning: new services, useful guides, community involvement, happy customers. Links that arrive for genuine reasons rarely cause problems, and they tend to last, unlike purchased links that often disappear or turn toxic.

The Bottom Line

Backlinks are recommendations written in the language of the web. They matter because Google cannot visit your shop, so it listens to what the rest of the internet says about you. Focus on being worth recommending, publish pages that help people, and let links follow from that. It is slower than buying them, but it is the only approach that keeps working year after year.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a backlink in plain language?

A backlink is a link on another website that leads to a page on your website. If a supplier's site links to your shop page, that link is a backlink for you and a normal outgoing link for them.

Do more backlinks always mean better rankings?

No. Quality and relevance matter far more than raw numbers. A few links from trusted, relevant websites usually help more than hundreds of links from low quality or unrelated sites, which Google may ignore or treat as spam.

Can backlinks harm my website?

Yes, links that are bought in bulk or placed on spammy sites can harm your visibility and may lead to penalties. Links earned naturally because your content is useful are safe and are the kind worth building.

How do I get my first backlinks?

Start with accurate listings on trusted directories, links from suppliers or partners you already work with, and helpful content that other sites want to reference. Ask politely and offer something useful in return, such as a genuinely helpful article.
