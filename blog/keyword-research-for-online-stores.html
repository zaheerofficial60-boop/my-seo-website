Keyword Research for Online Stores: Finding Searches That Buy

The right keywords tell you how shoppers actually describe your products and which of them are ready to buy. This guide shows store owners how to find those searches and match each one to the right page.

By Zaheer SEO Expert · 27 October 2026 · Ecommerce SEO

Every search a shopper makes is a clue. Some people are just learning, comparing ideas and gathering information. Others know exactly what they want and are choosing where to buy it. Keyword research for an online store means sorting those clues: discovering the words customers use for your products, judging which searches show buying intent, and making sure the right page in your store answers each one. Done well, it stops you guessing and starts you writing pages in your customers' own language.

This is a core part of our ecommerce SEO service, because almost every later decision, from category names to product titles, depends on getting these words right.

Start From Your Products and Your Customers

Begin with what you already know. List your main categories and best products, then write down how customers ask for them in real life: in your shop, on the phone and in messages. Customers rarely use the formal name a supplier prints on a box; they describe the item by its use, material, size or the problem it solves. Those everyday phrases are valuable starting keywords. Add the questions people ask before buying, because questions reveal searches your blog and guides can answer.

Then expand the list using free sources: the suggestions Google offers as you type a product name, the related searches at the bottom of results pages, and the search box inside large marketplaces where shoppers hunt for products like yours. Each source shows real language used by real buyers.

Recognize Buying Intent When You See It

Not all searches are equal for a store. Broad, general searches often belong to people still researching, while specific searches usually signal someone close to a purchase. Buying intent tends to show itself through details:

  • Specifics: a product type plus a size, colour, material, model or brand.
  • Action words: terms such as buy, price, order or delivery included in the search.
  • Comparisons: searches weighing two named products, made by someone nearing a decision.
  • Local intent: a product combined with a town or the words near me, where the shopper wants a reachable seller.

A small store should care most about these specific searches. They may individually bring fewer visitors than a famous broad term, but the visitors are far more likely to buy, and the competition is usually more beatable, as our guide for small online stores competing on Google explains.

Check the Competition Before You Commit

For each promising keyword, search it yourself and look honestly at what appears. If the results are dominated by huge marketplaces and the exact product is a commodity you cannot differentiate, that keyword may be a poor early target. If the results include ordinary stores with thin or outdated pages, a well-written page of yours has a fair chance. Also check that the results match what you sell: if Google shows guides and videos for a search, it is a research search, and it belongs on a blog article rather than a product page.

This step is judgement, not machinery. You are simply asking: can my page be one of the most useful answers to this search? If yes, keep the keyword. If not yet, note it for later.

Map Each Keyword to One Right Page

Research only helps when it is applied, and the application is called mapping: assigning each keyword group to the single page that should answer it. Specific product searches map to product pages. Searches for a type of product map to the category page for that type, which you can build using our category page SEO guide. Question and comparison searches map to blog articles that help the shopper decide, then link to the products discussed.

Mapping prevents a common store problem where several pages chase the same search and weaken each other. Keep a simple list, even in a notebook or spreadsheet: keyword, intent, and the page responsible for it. Update that list whenever you add products or categories, and use it when writing titles and descriptions so the whole store stays consistent.

Keep Learning From Your Own Search Data

Once your store is connected to Google Search Console, research becomes easier and more accurate, because you can see the actual searches that already show your pages. You will find phrases you never thought of, including the exact wording of buyers in your area. Feed those discoveries back into your product titles, category introductions and new articles. Keyword research is therefore not a one-time task at launch, but a habit that keeps your store speaking the same language as the people searching for what you sell.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a buying-intent keyword?

It is a search that shows the person is close to purchasing, such as a specific product name with a size, model or the word buy. These searches are usually more specific than general research searches, and they belong on product and category pages.

Do I need paid tools for keyword research?

Not to begin. Google suggestions, related searches, your own customer questions and Search Console data are free and often more relevant to a small store than generic lists. Paid tools can help later, but good thinking matters more than the tool.

Should every keyword get its own page?

No. Group keywords that mean the same thing and serve them with one strong page. Create separate pages only where the shopper's need is genuinely different, such as a distinct product type. Many thin pages are weaker than a few clear, complete ones.

How often should I repeat keyword research?

Review it whenever your product range changes and give it a proper refresh a few times a year. Customer language, seasons and stock all shift, and your Search Console data will show new searches appearing that your store can answer.
